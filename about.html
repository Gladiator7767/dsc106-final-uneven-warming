BEHIND THE COMPARISON

Data, methods
and project progress

This page supports Beyond the Global Average: Exploring Uneven Warming. The story compares conditional CMIP6 simulations for six latitude bands and a separate global reference.

Data and calculation

We use near-surface air temperature from three CMIP6 models—CanESM5, IPSL-CM6A-LR, and MPI-ESM1-2-LR—selecting one ensemble member from each model. We compare the two scenarios SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5 for the period 2015–2100. The six latitudinal bands include both land and ocean. All warming values are calculated relative to the 1995–2014 average temperature of the same model and region, not compared to pre-industrial conditions.

Regional averages are weighted by grid area, and annual averages are weighted by the number of days in each month. When calculating period averages and model averages, all years and models have equal weights. Global averages are computed separately, not by simply averaging the means of the six latitude bands. Scenario differences are first calculated within each individual model, then averaged across models. The ten-year average curves use data from the current year and the previous nine years, starting from 2024; smoothing only affects the curve shape, while period comparisons still use annual data.

  • Six latitude bands. 60–90°N, 30–60°N, 0–30°N, 0–30°S, 30–60°S and 60–90°S. Grid cells are assigned using their latitude centers. Land and ocean are included. Global is calculated over the full grid; it is not an unweighted mean of the six bands.
  • Scenario gap. SSP5-8.5 warming minus SSP1-2.6 warming, paired within the same model and period. This comparison is not a causal estimate of a specific policy and assigns no probability to either scenario.
  • Recent-history baseline. Temperature changes are relative to 1995–2014. A value of +2°C on this site is not the Paris Agreement's 2°C threshold.
  • Trailing average. Each 10-year displayed mean uses that year and the nine preceding years. Complete future-series windows begin in 2024. Period comparisons and CSV downloads always use original annual values.
  • Selection and precision. Three models were selected for data availability, not as a representative sample of all CMIP6 models. One realization per model cannot represent all internal variability. Cached annual anomalies are rounded to five decimal places; visible labels use two. Calculations and downloads use the cached values, not the rounded labels.

Why these views are linked

The article moves from a global reference to regional differences, then to scenarios and model disagreement. The initial prediction is always evaluated using 2081–2100, SSP5-8.5 and the mean of the three selected models. Later controls let the reader check other comparisons. The global and regional timelines use the same fixed vertical scale, while direct labels and distinct line styles support reading without color alone.

The equal-area locator map identifies the six bands. It does not encode warming through land area or imply city-level detail. The scenario-gap matrix uses a fixed diverging color scale centered at zero. Custom time windows are shown in the main charts; matrix cells always refer to the three labeled 20-year periods.

Initial prototype progress

Development update · November 13, 2026

What have we done so far?

We used the CMIP6 data verified in Project 3 and transformed the page into a sequential interactive story. Readers are first asked to guess which latitudinal band will warm the most, followed by displaying the comparison results. Then, readers can select a region and continue comparing global and regional trends, time periods, and models. Brief explanations link the charts together and distinguish warming from the differences between two scenarios. Next, we will revise the text and further refine it based on feedback from course staff before recording the final video.

What will be most challenging to design, and why?

The most challenging design issue is ensuring the story remains clear even when readers change several options. The regions, time periods, and models in charts and captions must remain consistent. Warming and scenario differences need to be clearly labeled separately, as they address different questions. We also want readers to see model differences, but without giving the impression that these three models encompass all possible outcomes. The opening prediction should connect smoothly to subsequent explorations, enabling readers to continue examining results rather than simply accepting a single revealed answer.
